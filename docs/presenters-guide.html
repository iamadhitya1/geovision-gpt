01 — Open with this

The pitch

Two versions — the one-breath version, and the one with substance underneath it if your guide leans in.

Ten seconds

"I built and trained my own visual question answering model for satellite images — not an existing AI model called through an API, but a model I assembled, trained on the institute's GPU cluster, and evaluated myself. And there's a working web app on top, so I can show it live."

If they lean in

"The architecture follows a published paper, LiT-4-RSVQA — a small pretrained image transformer plus BERT-Tiny for the question, fused, then classified into one of a thousand answers. It's about 8 million parameters, not billions, because that's what actually fits on a shared GPU in a semester. I reproduced the paper's design and got 75.2% average accuracy on data the model never saw. My own experiment was replacing the paper's simple fusion with cross-attention. It tied the baseline — 72.3% against 72.7% overall — so the richer fusion didn't measurably help. That's an honest result, and I can tell you about the first attempt, which failed for an interesting reason. I also measured how much of the score is just guessing from the wording of the question: a model that never sees the image gets 70.4%, so the image adds about two points overall, and clearly more on urban-versus-rural."

Say the ten-second version first, always. Only go to the second one if they ask a follow-up — don't front-load the technical version, it reads as reciting rather than understanding.

03 — Know this cold

How it works, end to end

One picture and six plain steps. If you can draw this from memory and say each step in a sentence, you can handle most questions.

  1. The picture goes in. The 256 × 256 satellite image is resized to 224 × 224 and cut into a 14 × 14 grid of small squares (196 patches). XCiT-Nano, a small pretrained "transformer" image model, turns each patch — plus one summary of the whole image — into a list of 128 numbers: 197 lists in all.
  2. The question goes in. The question is split into word pieces and BERT-Tiny turns each piece into 128 numbers, using the surrounding words for context.
  3. Fusion — the step I experimented with. Now the model must combine what it sees with what it was asked. A (baseline): squash each side into one list of 512 numbers and multiply the two together position by position. B (mine): let every word of the question look across all 197 image lists, weight the ones that matter to that word, and pull that information in.
  4. The classifier decides. A small network turns the fused numbers into 1,000 scores, one per possible answer. Softmax converts them to probabilities that add up to 100%. The app shows the top answer, its probability (the "confidence"), and the next-best answers.
  5. How it learned. It saw 57,223 training questions. For each, it guessed; a "loss" measured how far the guess was from the right answer; an optimiser (AdamW) nudged all ~8 million numbers a little (learning rate 5e-4). That happened 64 questions at a time, ten full passes ("epochs") over the data, about 73 minutes on one GPU slice. Nothing was frozen: the pretrained image and text models were fine-tuned too, which the paper found is worth about 13 points over starting from scratch. After every epoch it was scored on a separate validation set to watch for memorising.
  6. How it was tested. On 9,838 test questions that were never used to train it or to make any decision. The final-epoch model was scored once — no picking the best epoch on the test set.

How the web app fits around it

  • The browser (Next.js) signs you in and uploads an image.
  • The backend (FastAPI) checks the file really is the type it claims, reads its location data, stores it with a thumbnail, and remembers who owns it.
  • When you ask a question, the backend checks you own the image, runs both models (off the main thread so the server stays responsive), saves the question and both answers in the database, and returns them. About 0.4 seconds per model on a laptop CPU.
  • The map draws images at their real coordinates; the dashboard shows the measured accuracy and your usage.
  • Inside the backend, code is in four layers — domain rules, use cases, infrastructure, API — and a test fails the build if an inner layer ever depends on an outer one.

How to read it honestly

The two are effectively tied. Overall they differ by 0.4 points, about 40 questions out of 9,838. The bigger gap in average accuracy (3 points) comes almost entirely from rural/urban: it has only 100 test questions, but the average weights it as a quarter of the score, so the baseline getting 11 more of them right moves the average by nearly 3 points. On the three large categories the models are within about 1.3 points either way, with cross-attention slightly ahead on counting.

What you can and can't claim. You can say cross-attention did not measurably improve on the baseline in this setup, at about 13% more parameters. You can't say the baseline is better — the difference is inside what one training run can't distinguish. Counting is weak for both (about 28%), consistent with it being the hardest question type for small VQA models generally.

Against the paper. The paper reports 64.61%; your baseline is higher, but it trained on a much larger dataset (RSVQAxBEN, ~15M examples) and you trained on RSVQA-LR directly, so it isn't like-for-like. Don't claim to have beaten it.

What the image actually adds — the number to know

A predictor that never looks at the image and answers each question with the most common training answer to that exact question scores 70.42% overall — because RSVQA-LR's questions are templated. So both models beat it by only about two points. The image clearly matters for rural/urban (56% → 92% and 81%), adds a little on presence and comparison, and adds nothing on counting: both models are level with always saying "0". This is a known weakness of templated benchmarks, not of your implementation — but it is the honest floor to judge the models against, and an examiner may well ask. The dashboard shows it as a grey bar, and ml/scripts/question_only_baseline.py reproduces it in one command.

The first cross-attention attempt — the story to tell

What happened: the first run scored 36.06% overall (27.07% average), with rural/urban and counting both at exactly zero. Its validation accuracy sat on the same one or two values from epoch 0 to epoch 9 and the training loss never moved. That is the signature of a model giving nearly the same answer whatever it's asked.

The cause: a design bug of mine. I had squashed the image and the question to one vector each before the attention step, so attention ran over a single item — and softmax over one item is always exactly 1. The question had zero influence on the output. I proved it: changing the question changed the output by exactly 0.0, while changing the image changed it by 0.84.

The fix: keep both as sequences, so every question word attends over all image patches. Retrained: 36% → 72%. I also made the model choose its fusion in one place (three scripts had each swapped it in by hand, which is how it went unnoticed) and added an automated test that both the question and the image must change a fusion module's output.

Why tell it: a run that finishes without errors isn't proof the model works. Finding, proving and fixing this from a flat training curve shows you understand the model, not just the tooling.

08 — The "why," not just the "what"

Why this plan, and why these specific choices

A guide asking "why" is testing whether you understand the decisions or just followed a tutorial. Each of these has a one-line reason — lead with that, then expand only if asked.

Why the plan changed at all

Say this one plainly, it reflects well on you: the original plan called an existing vision-language model through an API and built a verification layer on top of it. Your guide reviewed that and said the model itself — training one and understanding its internals — is the actual point of the project, not the web app around it. The plan changed because that's the right call, not because the earlier work was wasted: the web app became the delivery layer for the trained model.

Why LiT-4-RSVQA specifically

Because it directly answers "can a model this small still be accurate?" — and the paper's own results say yes: 8.1M parameters beating a 277M-parameter baseline by roughly 9 accuracy points, and pretrained-then-fine-tuned beating training-from-scratch by nearly 13 points on the identical architecture. That second number is the reason this project fine-tunes pretrained encoders. It's also realistic for a semester on a shared GPU slice, which a multi-billion-parameter model is not.

Why RSVQA-LR, and not a bigger benchmark

Small enough to fully train and evaluate in the time and compute available, and it's the benchmark the paper itself reports on. Richer benchmarks now exist (see §10); moving to one is honest future work, not something overlooked.

What the original contribution is

The paper's design is the baseline, reproduced. The experiment is swapping its elementwise fusion for cross-attention on identical data, encoders and training settings, so any difference is attributable to that one change. The result was a tie — a clean negative result, reported as one.

Why a layered architecture in the web app

Four layers — domain rules, use cases, infrastructure, API — with inner layers forbidden from depending on outer ones, enforced by a test that fails the build. That's why the database can swap from SQLite to PostgreSQL, or the model runner can change, without touching business logic.

Why training ran on the institute HPC

No GPU on the laptop, and CPU training is impractical at this size. The cluster is used through SLURM after a formal declaration form. Everything was written and CPU-verified first so GPU time went to running working jobs. The real cluster still surfaced four problems no laptop could have (the reserved-GPU labelling, a driver/CUDA mismatch and a GPU-slice addressing problem that each made training silently fall back to the CPU, and an out-of-memory error), each diagnosed from the cluster's own output.

09 — For "do you actually understand VQA"

The VQA background

Visual Question Answering: a model is given an image and a question in natural language, and must produce an answer grounded in the image. This project frames it as classification — picking the right answer from the 1,000 most frequent in the training set — rather than generating free text. That's a standard framing in this literature, exactly how the original RSVQA benchmark and LiT-4-RSVQA both work, and it's why the app can't answer open-ended "describe this" questions.

The part worth being able to say clearly: satellite VQA is not just "VQA on different photos." It's a different problem, for four concrete reasons.

10 — Name these with confidence

Papers and datasets worth knowing cold

The core reading is the two surveys originally assigned — Vision-Language Modeling Meets Remote Sensing (Weng, Pang & Xia, IEEE GRSM, Sept 2025) and Visual Question Answering: A Survey of Methods, Datasets, Evaluation, and Challenges (Kim et al., ACM Computing Surveys, May 2025) — plus the paper this project's architecture follows, and a literature search covering 2024–2026.

LiT-4-RSVQAHackel, Clasen, Ravanbakhsh & Demir — TU Berlin, 2023 (arXiv:2306.00758) — the paper this project follows

BERT-Tiny text encoder, a lightweight pretrained image transformer (XCiT-Nano, DeiT-Tiny or MobileViT-S), elementwise-product fusion, MLP classifier. Best result — 64.61% average accuracy at 8.1M parameters, beating a 277M-parameter baseline by about 9 points. Its most important number for this project: the same architecture trained from scratch scores only 51.71% — nearly 13 points lower. That's the evidence behind fine-tuning rather than training from zero.

RSVQALobry, Marcos, Murray & Tuia — IEEE TGRS, Dec 2020

The founding benchmark this project trains and evaluates on (the low-resolution split, RSVQA-LR). ResNet-152 + skip-thought vectors, elementwise fusion — the direct ancestor LiT-4-RSVQA is a lightweight successor to.

GeoChatKuckreja et al. — CVPR 2024

Useful as contrast, not as a target: CLIP ViT-L/14 → MLP connector → Vicuna-v1.5-7B, fine-tuned with LoRA. Billions of parameters, generates free text rather than classifying. The honest answer to "why not build this" is scale — and that it isn't the depth-of-understanding exercise your guide asked for.

What's moved since 2023 — from a literature search run September 2026

The biggest systems keep getting bigger: RS-MoE (Lin et al., 2024) routes between expert LLMs; Co-LLaVA (Liu et al., 2025) combines two VLMs and beats GeoChat on RSVQA-LR itself; HSF-Net (Liu et al., 2026) adds hierarchical semantic fusion on top of LLaVA. All are billion-parameter systems — good to name for currency, not to chase.

One number that backs this project's premise: RS-MoE's 1B-parameter variant performs comparably to 13B-parameter VLMs — current, independent evidence that a small, efficient model can compete with much larger ones.

On benchmarks: EarthVQANet (Wang et al., 2024) describes RSVQA as covering "basic statistics for general objects," and the much larger RSVLM-QA (Zi et al., 2025 — 162,373 pairs vs. RSVQA-LR's 77,232) has since appeared. This project's scope is intentionally the simpler, established benchmark; richer ones are future work.

MQVQA (Zhang et al., 2023) is directly comparable prior work — a multi-step attention mechanism on the same benchmark family. And Lu et al.'s 2023 review of attention in VQA fusion is the citation for why testing cross-attention against the elementwise baseline is a literature-grounded experiment.

11 — Say these in plain words

Glossary

Each one is a definition you could say to a friend.

VQA (visual question answering)
Give a computer a picture and a question in ordinary language; it answers in ordinary language.
Parameter
One of the adjustable numbers inside a model. Learning means adjusting them. This model has about 8 million; large models have billions.
Vector
A list of numbers. Models turn pictures and words into vectors so they can compare and combine them.
Encoder
The part that turns raw input (an image or a sentence) into vectors the rest of the model can use.
Transformer
A kind of model built around "attention" — letting every piece of the input look at every other piece to decide what matters. XCiT-Nano and BERT-Tiny are both small transformers.
Patch / token
A small piece of the input: a 16 × 16 square of the image, or a word piece of the question.
Pretrained / fine-tuned
Pretrained: already taught general skills on other data (ImageNet photos, general text). Fine-tuned: then adjusted on this project's data. The paper found this beats starting from scratch by about 13 points.
Fusion
The step that combines what the image says with what the question asks.
Elementwise product
Multiplying two equal-length lists of numbers position by position. The simple fusion in the paper and in the baseline.
Attention / cross-attention
Attention: scoring how relevant each piece is, then taking a weighted mix. Cross-attention: the question's words score the image's patches, so each word pulls in the regions relevant to it.
Softmax / confidence
Softmax turns raw scores into probabilities that add to 100%. The top one is the "confidence". A high confidence is not a guarantee of being right.
Classification framing
Treating the answer as a choice from a fixed list (here, the 1,000 most common answers) instead of writing free text.
Loss (cross-entropy)
A single number for how wrong the model's guesses are. Training tries to make it smaller.
Epoch / batch
An epoch is one full pass over the training data. A batch is the handful of examples (64 here) processed before each adjustment.
Learning rate / AdamW
The learning rate is how big each adjustment is. AdamW is the standard method for deciding the adjustments.
Train / validation / test
Train: what it learns from. Validation: checked during training to spot memorising. Test: kept untouched until the end for the honest score.
Overfitting
Doing better on the training data while not improving (or getting worse) on new data — memorising instead of learning.
Overall vs. average accuracy
Overall: the plain share of questions answered correctly. Average: the mean of the four per-type accuracies, matching the paper. A small type like rural/urban (100 questions) has the same weight as a huge one.
Baseline
The reference you compare against. Here: the paper's design (elementwise fusion), and separately a "no image" guess.
Ablation / controlled experiment
Change one thing, keep everything else identical, see what moves. Swapping only the fusion is the project's experiment.
Language bias / no-image baseline
When the wording of a question already predicts the answer. A predictor that never sees the image and answers by wording alone scores 70.4% here.
Checkpoint
A saved snapshot of all the model's numbers. The app loads two of them.
HPC / SLURM / MIG
HPC: the institute's shared GPU cluster. SLURM: the scheduler you submit jobs to. MIG: splitting one big GPU into several smaller, separate slices.
GeoTIFF / CRS / GSD
GeoTIFF: an image file that also stores where on Earth it is. CRS: the coordinate system used (these files use Web Mercator). GSD: how much ground one pixel covers.
12 — Rehearse these out loud once

Questions to expect, and how to answer them

"Did cross-attention beat the baseline?"

No — it tied it. 72.3% against 72.7% overall on 9,838 held-out questions. It was slightly better at counting and slightly worse at rural/urban, but that category has only 100 test questions, so I don't read it as a real difference. Each model was trained once, so the honest conclusion is that the richer fusion didn't measurably help here, at about 13% more parameters. Repeating runs with different seeds would be the next step.

"Your first cross-attention run scored 36%. What happened?"

A bug in my own design. I'd reduced the image and the question to one vector each before attention, so it attended over a single item and softmax gave it a weight of exactly 1 — the question never reached the classifier. I spotted it from a training curve that never moved, proved it by showing the output changed by exactly zero when I changed the question, and fixed it by attending over all image patches. Retraining took it from 36% to 72%, and there's now a test that guards against it. Lead with what you noticed and how you proved it — the flat curve, the zero — not with the fact that it went wrong.

"Why is your accuracy higher than the paper's 64.6%?"

It isn't a fair comparison, and I don't claim it is. The paper trained on a much larger dataset (RSVQAxBEN, about 15 million examples) and reports on that setting; I trained and tested directly on RSVQA-LR. The number that matters is that my reproduction of their architecture works, and that the parameter count matches (8.2M vs. their 8.1M).

"Why not just fine-tune or call an existing model like GeoChat or Qwen2.5-VL?"

Two reasons. Scale: those are billion-parameter systems that need far more compute than a shared institute GPU realistically provides in a semester. And you were asked to understand a VQA model's internals, not call an API. A model this size can be trained, tested, debugged and fully explained end to end by one person; a 7B-parameter model can't, at this depth.

"You're following a published paper's architecture. What's actually yours?"

The paper's architecture is the baseline, reproduced honestly. The original piece is the cross-attention experiment on top — implemented, debugged, trained and measured against the paper's own baseline on identical data and encoders. I also built the pipeline, the training and evaluation code, the model-serving backend and the interface, and I can explain every component. Using pretrained weights isn't a shortcut around "making your own model" — it's how the field works, and the paper's own numbers show why.

"Why does a satellite VQA tool need authentication and admin roles?"

It was built under the original plan, and it turned out to be the right foundation: uploaded images and question history belong to a user, so the model service needs to know whose data it's touching. It has 111 tests and real security properties — Argon2id hashing, single-use refresh tokens, role-based access.

"You don't have a GPU. How did training happen?"

On the institute's HPC cluster through SLURM, after the formal declaration form. Every script was verified on CPU first so the GPU time ran working jobs. Even so, the real cluster exposed four problems no laptop could — the reserved-GPU labelling, a driver/CUDA mismatch and a GPU-slice addressing problem that each silently fell back to the CPU, and an out-of-memory error — each diagnosed from the cluster's own output.

"What happened to the SGV / spectral-verification idea from the original plan?"

Set aside when the priority moved to the model itself — checking a model's quantitative claims against spectral measurements doesn't apply the same way to a classifier that picks from a fixed answer list. Whether it returns as a layer later is genuinely open: an idea on hold, not one proven wrong. A guide respects an owned pivot far more than one glossed over — say plainly that the plan changed and why.

"What are the limits?"

It answers from a fixed list of 1,000 answers, so it can't write free text or answer questions outside that vocabulary. Counting is weak (about 28%). It's trained on one region and resolution (Sentinel-2 over the Netherlands), so it may not transfer elsewhere. Each model was trained once. And the test set, RSVQA-LR, is narrower than newer benchmarks like RSVLM-QA.

"How much of that accuracy is just guessing from the question?"

A lot of it, and I measured it. A predictor that never sees the image and answers with the most common training answer to each exact question scores 70.4% overall on the test set; my models score 72.7% and 72.3%. So the image adds about two points overall. It clearly matters for rural/urban — 56% without the image, 92% and 81% with it — helps a little on presence and comparison, and doesn't help counting at all. That's a known weakness of templated benchmarks like RSVQA, and it's why I show the no-image bar next to the models on the dashboard. Lead with it before you're asked. It turns the strongest objection into evidence that you evaluated the result.

"Why classification instead of generating a sentence?"

It's the standard framing for this benchmark and it's what the paper does. It makes accuracy exactly measurable, keeps the model small, and is honest about scope: the model picks from the 1,000 most common answers. The cost is that it can't answer open-ended questions, which I say up front.

"Why BERT-Tiny and XCiT-Nano?"

They're the paper's best small combination: BERT-Tiny is 2 layers and 128 numbers wide, XCiT-Nano is a small pretrained image transformer, and together the model is about 8 million parameters. Small enough to train in about an hour on one GPU slice and to run on a laptop CPU, which matters for a live demo.

"Explain cross-attention in one breath."

"Every word of the question looks across all the little squares of the image, scores how relevant each is to that word, and pulls in a weighted mix of them — so 'road' can focus on road-looking patches — instead of the image and the question each being squashed into one summary and multiplied."

"How do you know it isn't just memorising the training data?"

Two checks. Validation accuracy was scored after every epoch on data it wasn't training on, and the final score is on a separate test set used once. The baseline's training loss fell from 1.42 to 0.91 over ten epochs while validation accuracy stayed at about 73–74% — so after the first epoch it was mostly fitting the training set better, not generalising better, which is why I didn't run longer.

"Did you pick the best epoch using the test set?"

No. I evaluated the final epoch, once, on the test split. The validation set was only for watching training.

"Why batch 64, learning rate 5e-4, ten epochs?"

The learning rate is the paper's. The paper used batch 512, which ran out of memory on the roughly 10 GB GPU slice I had, so I used 64. I didn't tune anything, and both models use identical settings, which keeps the comparison fair. The catch: batch size differs from the paper, so my baseline isn't an exact replication.

"Which number should I quote — overall or average?"

Overall means "how often is it right" — 72.7% for the baseline. Average is the mean of the four question types, which is what the paper reports, so use it when comparing to the paper. It's less stable here because rural/urban has only 100 questions but counts as a quarter.

"The app says about 16 m per pixel but Sentinel-2 is 10 m. Which is it?"

Sentinel-2's native resolution here is about 10 m. The files are stored in Web Mercator, which stretches distances away from the equator by 1/cos(latitude), and at 51–53°N that turns 10 m into roughly 16–17 m — the app reports 15.8 m at 51.2°N and 16.5 m at 52.8°N, which matches. Say it as "the map projection stretches it", not as a data problem.

"Would it work on other cities or other satellites?"

Untested. It was trained on Sentinel-2 imagery of the Netherlands with templated questions, so I'd expect it to need fine-tuning for other sensors, resolutions or regions. I'd say that plainly rather than guess.

"Did you use AI tools to build this?"

Answer truthfully and within your institute's rules. If it's accurate for you: "Yes — I used an AI coding assistant for implementation. I made the decisions — the pivot, the model choice, the cross-attention experiment, reporting the tie honestly — and I can explain, run and reproduce every part; I found and diagnosed the cross-attention bug from the training curve." Then prove it by explaining something. Never claim to have hand-written what you didn't.

"What would you do next?"

Repeat each run with several random seeds to measure run-to-run noise; move to a larger benchmark such as RSVLM-QA; visualise which patches each word attends to, to see if cross-attention learns something sensible; try to fix counting, which is stuck at the no-image level; and compare against a larger model to put a number on the small-model trade-off.
